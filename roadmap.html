    Methods that compound

    Learn faster

    Hours matter less than what you do inside them. These are the habits that change how much sticks.

    Recall, do not re-read

    Close the notes and write what you remember, then check. Re-reading feels smooth and teaches little. Every revision block is a re-solve from a blank file.

    Space it: 1, 3, 7, 21 days

    Re-solve each problem a day later, then at 3, 7 and 21 days. If it takes under 10 minutes twice, retire it. If you fail, the clock restarts.

    Learn patterns, not problems

    After each problem, write one line: the trigger that should have told you the technique. "Sorted array plus a target: two pointers or binary search." Interviews test the trigger.

    Keep an error log

    One line per mistake: off-by-one, overflow, wrong base case, misread constraint. Read it before every test. Most people repeat the same five errors.

    Struggle on purpose

    The 30 minutes before a hint are where the learning happens. If you never get stuck, the problems are too easy; if you are always stuck, drop a level.

    Explain it out loud

    Say the approach, the complexity and one edge case aloud before coding. If you cannot explain it simply, you do not know it yet. This is also exactly what an interview is.

    Read constraints first

    n up to 10⁵ means O(n log n). n up to 20 means bitmasks or backtracking. The constraints often name the algorithm before you have read the story.

    Practise under the clock

    From week 6, one timed set a week: 3 problems in 90 minutes, no pausing. Speed and nerves are skills, and they only train under time.

    Mix topics once you know them

    Learning a topic: do it in a block. Revising: shuffle topics, so you practise choosing the technique, which is the hard part in a test.

    Two deep blocks beat ten shallow hours

    Two 2-hour blocks with the phone in another room, hardest work first. Take a 10-minute break every 50 minutes. Put easy work (videos, AWS) late in the day.

    Protect sleep and movement

    Seven to eight hours of sleep is when memory consolidates. A 20-minute walk a day keeps you sharp. Cutting sleep to study loses more than it gains.

    Review weekly, in writing

    Every Sunday, 20 minutes: what was solved, what failed and why, what to change. Then plan the week. Without the review you repeat the week instead of improving it.

    For theory: questions, not notes

    Turn every Core CS topic into 5 questions and answer them from memory. NotebookLM can quiz you. Notes you never test yourself on are decoration.

    Build to understand

    A linked list you implemented, a tiny LRU cache, a rate limiter: one small build per topic makes the idea permanent and gives you something to talk about.

    The complete system

    Off-campus

    Off-campus hiring is a numbers game played with a good profile. Expect roughly 100 applications for 5 to 10 tests and 2 to 4 interviews. Referrals change those odds more than anything else.

    Where openings appear

    • Company careers pages: check your 40-company list twice a week
    • LinkedIn Jobs, with alerts for "SDE intern", "software engineer 2028", "security intern"
    • Fresher programmes and hiring challenges run by large companies
    • Coding contests and hackathons that end in interviews
    • Startup boards such as Wellfound and Y Combinator's Work at a Startup
    • Alumni and seniors: the fastest route, through a referral

    Your profile, before the first application

    • Resume: one page, PDF, no photo. Order: education, projects, skills, achievements
    • Each bullet: what you built, with what, and a number. "Cut IAM policies from N per team to 2 using ABAC; 14 of 14 isolation tests pass."
    • GitHub: 4 pinned repos, each with a README, a diagram and how to run it
    • LinkedIn: a headline that says what you do, an About section, the projects, the AWS certification when you have it
    • Coding profiles: LeetCode and Codeforces links, once the numbers are worth showing

    The weekly rhythm

    • Monday: 30 minutes scanning your sources; shortlist 5 to 10 roles
    • Tuesday: apply, tailoring the top 3 resume bullets to each role
    • Wednesday: 5 referral messages
    • Friday: follow up on anything a week old, once
    • Always: log every application: company, role, link, date, referral, status, next step
    • Never pay anyone for a job, an interview or a "training bond". Real companies do not charge.

    Referral message to an alumnus

    Hi [Name], I'm a third-year CSE (Cybersecurity) student at RAIT, graduating in 2028. I saw the [role] opening at [company] (job ID [ID]).

I've built TenantWall, a multi-tenant access-control system on AWS that our team wrote up as a paper, and I've solved [N] DSA problems on LeetCode.

Would you be willing to refer me? My resume is attached, and I can send anything else you need. Thank you for your time.

    Keep it under 100 words. Name the exact role and job ID. Send it to people one to four years ahead of you; they remember the search.

    Message to a recruiter or engineer you do not know

    Hi [Name], I'm a 2028 CSE graduate from RAIT focused on backend and cloud security. I built [one-line project with a number] and hold [AWS certification, once earned].

I'm interested in [role] at [company]. Is there an intern or new-graduate opening I should apply to, or someone I should speak with? Resume attached.

    Expect about 1 reply in 10. That is normal. Follow up once after a week, then move on.

    In the interview

    • Think aloud from the first second; the interviewer is grading your reasoning
    • Ask for the constraints and an example before writing anything
    • State the complexity without being asked
    • When stuck, say what you are considering and why it fails; hints are part of the process
    • Test with a small case and an edge case before saying "done"
    • End with one real question about the team's work

    After every test or interview

    • Write down every question the same day
    • Solve the ones you missed within 48 hours
    • Add the gap to that week's plan
    • Rejections are data, not verdicts: most strong candidates collect dozens
    • With an offer: get it in writing, check the role, location, pay and joining date, and ask the college about the NOC before accepting

    Decided in advance

    Rules

    These settle the hard calls before the bad day arrives.

    Minimum day, never zero

    On a bad day: revision, 2 Striver problems, 1 LeetCode problem and 20 minutes of Core CS flashcards. A missed day must not become a missed week.

    When time is short, cut in this order

    1. HFT preparation
    2. CP-31
    3. AI, down to 1 hour
    4. Cyber, down to Sundays only

    Never cut DSA, revision or Core CS.

    Think before you look

    30 minutes alone before a hint. 60 before a solution. Then close it, code it from memory, and re-solve it the next day.

    Videos at the right moment

    Concept videos before a new topic, at 2×. Solution videos only after your own attempt.

    AI is a tutor, not an answer machine

    Ask for hints, reviews and quizzes. Never on a first attempt, never in a gate test. In projects, read and understand every line it writes.

    Limits

    Stop at 9 hours. Take one half-day off every week.

    If Striver is below Step 8 on 13 Dec

    Start AI at 1 hour a day in January, not 2.

    If Striver is not finished on 15 Feb

    Pause CP-31 until it is.

    Still open

    Decisions that change the plan

    • What is the minimum attendance your college requires? You plan to attend 1 to 2 days a week from 14 Dec. Check the rule for sitting exams and for placement eligibility, and stay just above it.
    • When is the TenantWall deadline, and where will it be submitted? Ask the professor. If it is before mid-December, the four Saturdays move earlier.
    • What did the professor mean by "something explicit"? Most likely explicit deny. Confirm before writing the section.
    • Which companies count as "good" for an NOC? The college allows an internship at a good company. Ask the placement office for the criteria before you apply, so every application is one you could accept.
